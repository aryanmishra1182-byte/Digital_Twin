An Explainable Digital Twin Framework for Predictive Maintenance of Three Phase Induction Motors
Three phase induction motors are used extensively in manufacturing, where a sudden breakdown can halt production and drive up maintenance expenditure. Run to failure repair gives almost no advance notice, and fixed interval servicing may swap out parts that still have useful life left. Predictive maintenance (PdM) tackles both problems by basing maintenance decisions on the measured condition of the machine. Earlier studies have produced digital twins, fault classification models, and explainable AI (XAI); however, these strands are usually pursued in isolation. This work proposes a framework that brings them together for three phase induction motors in one edge to dashboard pipeline. It pairs vibration, stator current, and temperature sensing with edge preprocessing, a staged machine learning pipeline for anomaly detection, fault classification, and remaining useful life (RUL) estimation, and a SHAP based explanation layer. The planned predictions and explanations are synchronized with a digital twin and shown on an operator dashboard. The paper surveys related work, pinpoints the architectural gap, describes the proposed system together with its signal processing relations, and sets out an evaluation plan that treats published results as external reference points. Because the architecture is modular, moving it to other rotating machines chiefly involves changing the feature extraction method and the fault taxonomy.
Keywords: Digital Twin; Explainable AI; SHAP; Predictive Maintenance; Three Phase Induction Motor; Motor Current Signature Analysis; Remaining Useful Life; Fault Diagnosis
I. Introduction
Three phase induction motors serve as the main drive source in a great deal of industrial equipment, including pumps, fans, compressors, conveyors, and machine tools. Their rugged, uncomplicated design and modest price suit them to many uses; even so, a motor breakdown can still stop the process it drives. Reactive, run to failure maintenance gives very little notice before a breakdown occurs. Fixed interval preventive maintenance covers part of the problem; however, it may retire components while they still have useful life and may overlook faults that emerge between planned inspections.
Predictive maintenance (PdM) relies on machine measurements to judge present condition and to spot early signs of degradation ahead of failure. A digital twin is a natural fit for this approach, since it keeps a virtual counterpart of the physical asset and refreshes it as new sensor readings come in. In the proposed system, the twin acts as the shared location where machine state, model outputs, and explanations are stored and shown. Tao et al. cast this concept for prognostics and health management as a five dimensional model covering the physical entity, the virtual counterpart, twin data, connections, and services [1]. Toothman et al. subsequently demonstrated that a state based digital twin framework can be reused over a fleet of mechanical assets instead of being built anew for every machine [2]. This study concentrates on induction motors because their principal electrical and mechanical failure modes (stator winding faults, broken rotor bars, bearing degradation, and air gap eccentricity) have well established diagnostic signatures in the motor current signature analysis (MCSA) literature [7]. These physical relationships give the feature engineering stage a grounded starting point.
Explainability is a further practical need. A maintenance engineer will find it hard to act on an output such as “failure in 40 hours” unless the system also shows the evidence behind it. SHAP (SHapley Additive exPlanations) attributes a contribution to each input feature, allowing the drivers of an individual prediction to be inspected [11]. Abood et al. have shown its use for induction motor diagnosis, applying SHAP on a SCADA connected testbed and finding that stator current distortion and torque oscillation were among the features that influenced the model output most [10]. From the literature examined in this paper, we found no single framework that unites multi sensor acquisition, a staged anomaly detection/classification/RUL pipeline, a synchronized digital twin, and SHAP based explanation specifically for induction motors. This combination constitutes the architectural gap that the proposed framework targets.
The paper offers four contributions. First, it specifies an edge to dashboard architecture in which a digital twin hosts a staged pipeline of anomaly detection, fault classification, and RUL estimation for three phase induction motors. Second, it defines a physics guided feature set based on MCSA sideband and bearing frequency relations, so that every feature carries a physical meaning that an engineer can verify. Third, it embeds SHAP explanations in the twin, so each alarm is displayed alongside the features responsible for it. Fourth, it sets out an evaluation plan that keeps simulated, bench, and published benchmark evidence apart, and it indicates which modules would need to change when the framework is applied to other rotating machines.
The rest of the paper is structured as follows. Section II surveys related work on digital twins, induction motor fault diagnosis, and explainable AI. Section III states the problem and the research gap. Section IV describes the proposed system architecture. Section V covers the machine learning methodology and the fault frequency relationships behind feature engineering. Section VI gives the evaluation plan and expected outcomes. Section VII considers practical issues and limitations. Section VIII explains how the architecture can be adapted to other rotating machines, and Section IX concludes the paper.
II. Related Work
A. Digital Twins for Predictive Maintenance
The five dimensional digital twin model of Tao et al. [1] supplies the physical, data, connection, virtual, and service elements found in many digital twin applications, including the structure used in this framework. Toothman et al. dealt with a practical drawback of one off digital twins, namely that health monitoring solutions tend to be built for a single machine and are hard to reuse. Their state based framework standardizes how health models are organized and how they communicate across a fleet of manufacturing assets [2]. Newer studies have linked digital twins to machine learning models for remaining useful life (RUL) estimation. Han et al. paired a digital twin with machine learning to monitor logistics equipment; they reported bearing assembly RUL predictions within about 10% tolerance on held out cases and also looked at motor winding degradation through thermal and electrical trends [4]. Wu et al. examined equipment RUL prediction more generally with a digital twin based pipeline [5]. These works back the digital twin plus ML approach adopted here, yet they lack the same pairing of induction motor specific validation with an explicit explainability layer.
B. Induction Motor Fault Diagnosis with Machine Learning
Kumar and Hati’s survey of machine learning based induction motor fault detection [8] outlines the main sensing modalities, namely vibration and stator current, together with the less common temperature and acoustic emission measurements. In later work they proposed a dilated convolutional neural network that detects bearing faults and broken rotor bars in squirrel cage induction motors using vibration data [9]. This result indicates that one learned model can separate several induction motor fault classes, so diagnosis need not be limited to a binary decision. Motor current signature analysis (MCSA), described by Thomson and Fenger, offers a non invasive means of identifying several induction motor faults from the stator current spectrum [7]. In the proposed framework, this current domain information is one of the principal feature sources in the feature extraction stage. Outside induction motors, Xiao et al. framed bearing fault detection as a graph neural network problem and reported a 6.4% AUC gain over the next best baseline on public bearing datasets [6]. This shows that the classification stage is still an active research area and could be swapped out without altering the rest of the proposed architecture.
C. Explainable AI in Fault Diagnosis
SHAP [11] gives each input feature a Shapley value that expresses its contribution to a specific prediction, offering a way to see which features drive the model output. Abood et al. constructed a real time, SCADA connected three phase induction motor testbed and applied a GRU classifier with SHAP explanations to tell genuine stator and rotor faults apart from anomalies caused by cyberattacks. They reported an overall classification accuracy of 93.17% and named packet delay, current total harmonic distortion, and torque oscillation as key contributing features [10]. Their study demonstrates SHAP in practice on an induction motor platform; however, it concentrates on cyber physical security rather than on a general predictive maintenance framework. The present work carries the same explainability idea over to the anomaly classification RUL pipeline detailed in Section V.

III. Problem Statement and Research Gap
Three phase induction motors can fail through several well characterized mechanisms, yet maintenance is still frequently driven by reactive repair or fixed inspection intervals. Reference [3] reported that roughly 32% of induction motor damage stems from insulation failure that leads to short circuited faults, and that about 80% of stator electrical faults are linked to weak turn to turn insulation. Such fault mechanisms can develop slowly and may therefore give measurable warning before a major failure. Bearing wear, rotor bar cracking caused by repeated thermal and electromagnetic stress, and air gap eccentricity add further degradation paths, each with distinct sensor signatures such as vibration, current sidebands, or thermal drift [3], [7].
The gap addressed here is mainly architectural, not the invention of a new learning algorithm. Existing digital twin predictive maintenance frameworks [1], [2] cover broader classes of equipment and do not include the full explainability layer proposed here. Induction motor classifiers [8], [9] typically handle a specific detection or classification task instead of a complete anomaly to RUL workflow embedded in a digital twin. Abood et al. [10] joined an induction motor classifier with SHAP, but their application centered on separating cyberattack induced anomalies from genuine faults instead of general mechanical and electrical predictive maintenance. Within the literature reviewed for this study, we found no single induction motor framework that combines (i) multi sensor acquisition, (ii) a staged anomaly detection → classification → RUL pipeline, (iii) a synchronized digital twin, and (iv) SHAP based explanation. This combination is therefore treated as the design gap under investigation.
IV. Proposed System Architecture
Fig. 1 illustrates the proposed end to end pipeline. Three sensing modalities monitor the motor: a vibration accelerometer for mechanical faults such as bearing wear, imbalance, and eccentricity; a current transformer or Hall effect current sensor for motor current signature analysis and for electrical or rotor faults; and a temperature sensor for thermal effects such as insulation aging. An edge device, for example an ESP32 or Raspberry Pi class controller, carries out noise filtering, buffering, and lightweight preprocessing prior to forwarding the data over the MQTT protocol to a time series data store. This keeps the communication link fairly light and lets the system keep running when the wide area connection is unreliable.
Fig. 1. Proposed sensor to dashboard pipeline for explainable digital twin predictive maintenance of three phase induction motors.
Once the data are stored, the feature extraction stage computes MCSA spectral features from the current signal, statistical and time frequency features from vibration, and rate of change features from temperature. The resulting features pass through three ML stages. The first flags departures from normal operation, the second assigns a fault class to any detected anomaly, and the third estimates remaining useful life from the sequence of observed features. The SHAP layer is applied to the classification and RUL stages so that every prediction can come with feature level contributions. The digital twin merges the incoming sensor data, model outputs, and SHAP values to update the virtual state of the motor. The dashboard then shows the current health score, condition label, RUL estimate, and the main factors behind the maintenance recommendation.
V. Methodology
A. Data Acquisition and Simulation
Since labeled fault data from operating production motors are hard and costly to obtain, the first dataset will be produced from a MATLAB/Simulink model of a three phase squirrel cage induction motor. The model is planned to cover healthy operation together with injected cases of broken rotor bars, stator inter turn shorts, bearing defects, and eccentricity, following the simulation approach described in [12]. The simulated dataset will support the initial development and validation of the pipeline, after which the models are meant to be moved to a physical bench setup fitted with the three sensors described in Section IV. The proposed configuration at present uses a 3 kW, 415 V, 50 Hz, four pole squirrel cage motor with a rated speed of about 1440 rpm (slip of about 4% at full load). Runs are planned at 25%, 50%, 75% and 100% of rated load, with two or three severity levels for each fault (for example one or two broken rotor bars, and roughly 2% to 10% of stator turns shorted). Current is sampled at 10 kHz and split into one second windows. At 4% slip, the broken rotor bar sidebands given by Eq. (1) lie close to 46 Hz and 54 Hz, which a one second window (1 Hz resolution) can resolve from the 50 Hz supply line. Vibration and bearing behaviour are harder to reproduce in an electrical Simulink model, so the vibration channel may be drawn from a mechanical sub model or supplemented with a public bearing dataset such as the CWRU bearing data; the exact source will be settled during implementation.

C. Staged Machine Learning Pipeline
Stage 1 will start with Isolation Forest as a lightweight baseline anomaly detector and will be compared against an autoencoder on the validation set. Stage 2 will start with XGBoost as the baseline classifier and will be compared against a 1 D CNN. The final model for each stage will be chosen on validation performance, computational requirements, stability across random seeds, and fit with the intended edge to dashboard deployment. Stage 3 will use an LSTM regressor for RUL estimation.

D. Explainability Layer
SHAP values will be computed for the Stage 2 classifier and the Stage 3 regressor with an explainer matched to the chosen model. TreeExplainer will be used if the gradient boosted classifier is chosen, while gradient or deep explainer methods can serve for CNN or LSTM variants. This approach follows the general SHAP formulation in [11] and its use for induction motor fault classification in [10]. For every prediction, the dashboard is designed to show the features with the largest contributions, such as a broken rotor bar sideband amplitude or vibration RMS near BPFO, alongside the predicted fault type. The aim is to give the maintenance engineer a physical basis for interpreting the model output instead of a bare probability value.
E. Digital Twin Synchronization
Following the five dimensional model in [1], the digital twin holds a reference to the physical motor, a virtual representation of its present health state and fault probabilities, a time series data layer, an MQTT based connection layer, and a service layer that contains the ML pipeline and SHAP explainer. As each new batch of features arrives, the virtual state is refreshed so that the health score tracks recent sensor evidence instead of a fixed snapshot.
VI. Evaluation Plan
Classification performance will be assessed with per class precision, recall, and F1 score on a held out test set. RUL performance will be assessed with root mean square error against the known degradation trajectory in the simulated dataset. Values reported in related studies will serve only as external reference points. Xiao et al. reported a 6.4% AUC gain over the next best bearing fault detection baseline with a graph neural network formulation [6]; Han et al. reported bearing assembly RUL predictions within about 10% tolerance over 87 test cases using a digital twin coupled model [4]; and Abood et al. reported 93.17% overall classification accuracy, with SHAP identified contributing features, on a real three phase induction motor testbed [10]. These published figures will be used for comparison with the later test and validation results of the proposed pipeline; they are not results obtained from the current project.
To keep the evaluation free of leakage, data will be split by simulation run and operating condition rather than by individual window, since windows from the same run are nearly identical and would leak between training and test sets. Generalization will also be tested by holding out one load level at a time. Simple baselines, such as a random forest and an SVM on the same features, will be reported next to the selected models, along with a confusion matrix so that confusion between faults with similar signatures (for example rotor bar and eccentricity faults) can be seen. Results will be averaged over several random seeds. Because RUL labels in simulation come from an assumed degradation curve, the RUL error will be read only relative to that assumed trajectory and not as evidence of real world prognostic accuracy; the physical bench setup is meant to test this further.

VII. Practical Considerations and Limitations
Data privacy and security: Motor telemetry sent over MQTT to a cloud or on premises store should be authenticated and encrypted. The cyber physical setting examined in [10] also illustrates why the communication link has to be treated as part of the system’s security boundary.
Implementation cost: Installing sensors and edge hardware and integrating them with existing SCADA infrastructure demand an upfront investment. In practice, this cost should be weighed against the operational gains from earlier fault detection.
Model trust and validation: SHAP explanations should likewise be checked against domain expectations before being used to support maintenance decisions. An explanation that looks plausible but is physically wrong could give operators unwarranted confidence in the model.
Data scarcity: Real fault data from production motors are scarce, which motivates the initial use of simulated fault injection [12]. When the models move to physical hardware, domain adaptation or extra calibration may be needed to account for differences between simulated and real operating conditions.
VIII. Generalization to Other Machine Types
The architecture in Fig. 1 separates the induction motor specific parts from the reusable ones. The feature extraction module in Section V B and the fault taxonomy used by Stage 2 are motor specific. By contrast, the sensing to edge to MQTT to store path, the anomaly detection/classification/RUL structure, the SHAP layer, and the digital twin/dashboard layers can be kept for other machines. This kind of reuse is also in line with the wider digital twin fault diagnosis literature [3].
As an example, adapting the framework to a centrifugal pump would mean replacing the MCSA features with vibration and pressure features tied to cavitation and flow, and replacing the fault taxonomy with classes such as impeller wear, seal leakage, and cavitation. The anomaly detection, RUL, SHAP, and dashboard layers can stay largely as they are. Turbines and permanent magnet synchronous motors need similar changes, since their relevant signatures and fault classes differ [3]. The proposed architecture is therefore intended as a reusable predictive maintenance structure and not a system restricted to one machine type.

IX. Conclusion
This study presents a proposed explainable digital twin framework for predictive maintenance of three phase induction motors. The design merges multi sensor acquisition, staged anomaly detection, fault classification, RUL estimation, SHAP based explanations, and a synchronized digital twin into a single operator facing pipeline. The present contribution is architectural and methodological: it defines the proposed sensor setup, physics guided features, candidate ML models, evaluation protocol, and development path, with no claim of experimental performance. The architecture is also meant to adapt to other rotating machines by changing the feature extraction and fault taxonomy modules. The next phase will generate the Simulink dataset, compare the candidate models under leakage controlled evaluation, integrate SHAP and the digital twin state update, and then assess the design on physical bench data where available.
